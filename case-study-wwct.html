Walk Wheel Cycle Trust

The Approach

Research-led from the start, with pan-impairment accessibility as a structural constraint not a layer on top. Rather than recruiting a one-off user cohort for end-stage testing, we built an embedded expert panel of charity partners and volunteers with lived experience of disability. The panel reviewed designs with us dynamically throughout the project rather than at fixed milestones.

This matters because pan-impairment accessibility work tends to fail at the seams: a design that works for one impairment type can create friction for another, and those tensions only surface when expertise is in the room continuously, not just at sign-off.

The Team

UX Lead (me), a supporting UI Designer I worked closely with through iterative design rounds, a development team, and a Project Manager on both sides. The client was the Walk Wheel Cycle Trust (formerly Sustrans). Pan-impairment research and design review was led in partnership with Transport for All and additional charity-sector partners, with an embedded expert panel of disabled volunteers reviewing work dynamically alongside us throughout the project.

The Problem

The Walk Wheel Cycle Trust is a UK charity advocating for active travel: walking, wheeling, cycling, and everything in between. Their remit covers users that mainstream mapping tools systematically fail.

We confirmed this by auditing the five most-used tools in the market (Google Maps, Apple iOS Maps, OS Maps, Strava, Komoot) against accessibility-critical functionality. Each fell short in different ways. Wheelchair routing was inaccurate. Offline access sat behind paywalls. Fitness filters used exclusionary language. Audio navigation didn't flag hazards.

The challenge: design a mapping tool that genuinely supports active travel for users mainstream tools overlook, without falling into the same trap of locking accessibility-critical features behind paywalls or assumed ability.

Method

Discovery (pan-impairment research with advocacy partners, stakeholder immersion, comparator analysis), definition (personas, design principles), and design through to prototype handover. Accessibility was woven through each phase rather than treated as a final QA step.

Discovery

Three strands ran in parallel.

Pan-impairment research and embedded expert review

Working alongside Transport for All and other charity-sector partners, we built a foundational picture of the barriers and frustrations users face when trying to engage with active travel. Crucially, the same network supported an ongoing expert panel that reviewed designs as they evolved. Findings didn't just feed into a discovery document and then sit on a shelf, they kept shaping decisions through definition and design.

Stakeholder immersion at the Trust

Listened to internal teams to understand the wider organisation, what it does, what it advocates for, and where the mapping tool needed to sit within that. Surfaced complexities like balancing the strength of the NCN brand against the wider organisational mission, and a historical service-delivery focus that had underrepresented end beneficiaries.

Comparator tool analysis

Five major mapping tools assessed against accessibility-critical functionality: audio navigation depth, offline access, terrain and hazard data, personalisation language, wheelchair routing accuracy. Four findings shaped the design direction:

  • Estimated journey durations are unreliable when tools don't account for terrain or user-specific pace.
  • Audio navigation needs to convey hazards and barriers, not just turn-by-turn directions.
  • Personalisation done badly (e.g. Komoot's "In Good Shape" fitness slider) excludes rather than includes.
  • Offline access is a safety must-have, never a premium feature.

Definition

Synthesised research and comparator analysis into five pan-impairment personas: Sarah, partially sighted, walking and running. Samuel, wheelchair user, integrating active travel into work-from-home life. Lucy, sensory-overwhelm and anxiety, walking commute. David, hand cyclist, fitness-driven. Kathy, new tricycle user, low digital confidence.

The personas were deliberately built to surface conflicting needs, not consensus. Designing for Sarah's audio navigation could create friction for Lucy's sensory overload. David's appetite for road traffic context conflicts with Kathy's need for safety reassurance. Each persona shipped with explicit "do this / don't do this" guidance, making them a working design tool rather than a marketing artefact.

Design

Worked collaboratively with the supporting UI Designer through iterative rounds, with each significant decision reviewed by the expert panel before it solidified. Three design decisions defined the tool.

A barrier map, not a routing tool

Existing mapping tools optimise for "fastest route" and bury accessibility filters behind premium accounts or vague fitness language. We deliberately stepped away from suggesting routes at all. The map surfaces community-reported barriers (path width, gates, steep gradients, surface quality) and lets users decide for themselves whether a route works for their needs. When the comparator analysis showed that Komoot's "In Good Shape" fitness slider was actively exclusionary, replacing it with another version of the same logic felt wrong. Trusting users with the raw information instead felt right.

Lightweight community data maintenance

Each barrier point shows its last-reported date. When a user selects one, they're prompted to confirm whether the barrier is still present. "Yes, it is" updates the timestamp. "No, it's gone" removes it. New points only appear on the map after being reported by more than one user, guarding against single-source noise. This pattern means the Trust can keep the map fresh through its volunteer network without needing centralised manual audits.

Accessibility patterns built into the persistent UI

Text size and contrast controls sit in the main interface, not buried in a settings menu. Filter information surfaces on both hover and tab so keyboard and mouse users see the same content at the same trigger. Help icons expand inline rather than navigating users away from what they were doing. The first-load warning modal is fully tab-able and users must confirm they understand the data's limitations before continuing, an honest contract rather than a hidden T&C.

Route discovery with pan-impairment filters and detail views, letting users assess suitability from community data rather than algorithmic route-suggesting.

Barrier reporting and inline help: community-fed data with lightweight maintenance built into the core experience.

Delivery

The prototype was built in Figma at sufficient fidelity that the Trust could extend it directly into user testing with their NCN volunteer network, hundreds of community members already engaged with active travel infrastructure on the ground. This was a deliberate handover model. Rather than the Trust waiting on us for the next round of validation, the prototype embedded a research practice into the organisation that could continue running after our involvement ended.

Outcome

The project achieved what discovery-through-design work is meant to achieve: it built enough evidence and clarity to validate the direction of the tool and the methodology behind it.

Alongside the prototype handover, we produced a findings report documenting the pan-impairment research, the comparator analysis, the personas, and the design rationale. This means the work outlasts the project itself. The personas remain a working reference for any future product or campaign decisions the Trust makes for disabled users, and the design principles (community-fed data, barrier mapping over route-suggesting, structural accessibility) are documented in a form that can guide the next team picking up the build.

The expert panel model also has legs beyond this engagement. Embedding lived-experience review throughout, rather than as one-off user testing, is a methodology the Trust can carry into future projects with or without us.
