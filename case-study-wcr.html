The Approach

My approach to this project was research-led from the start. Rather than moving straight to design, I invested time upfront in understanding the charity's internal landscape, its users, and the wider market. Each phase informed the next, with insights from discovery directly shaping the definition work, and definition directly shaping design decisions. Keeping stakeholders involved and informed throughout meant that by the time we reached build, there was strong buy-in and a clear, validated direction to work from.

The Team

This project ran with myself as lead UX Designer, a UI Designer, a team of two Developers (one front-end, one back-end) and a Project Manager. I worked collaboratively with all involved throughout, working most closely with a supporting UI designer. A third party was used for functional, accessibility and QA testing.

The Problem

As with any large charity, change would require buy-in from a great number of stakeholders, each with their own agenda to be carefully managed. In some cases our research and definition needed to challenge preconceived ideas about what their website could do, what it couldn't, and what users actually needed versus internal assumptions.

Two of the biggest challenges were communicating the story of discovery research clearly without losing user interest, and demonstrating impact when discovery research can take decades to translate into a new treatment or cure.

Method

This project followed a full end-to-end process across five phases.

Discovery

Discovery involved stakeholder interviews, competitor analysis, and thematic research synthesis.

Definition

Definition covered user journey mapping, sitemap design, and persona development informed by both our research and the charity's own brand and marketing work.

Development

Development ran as a collaborative process with the front-end and back-end team, with design feeding directly into build.

Testing was handled by a third-party specialist covering functional, accessibility, and QA.

Post-launch

The project moved into an ongoing digital partnership post-launch, with continued optimisation work carried out in the years since.

Discovery

Worldwide Cancer Research had recently undergone a complete brand refresh, the kind of change that raises expectations internally as much as externally. It was the charity's first major digital overhaul in some time, which meant a large cohort of stakeholders had a stake in the outcome. Managing that without letting internal opinion drown out user needs was a central challenge from day one.

I planned and ran a stakeholder interview programme, pairing participants two-per-session to keep the process manageable without losing depth. The interviews surfaced both recognised challenges and blind spots, things stakeholders hadn't considered the website could address. Running them this way also helped build buy-in early, so the strategy phase had a coalition behind it rather than a room full of competing agendas.

Alongside this, I ran a competitor analysis to understand what was working in the wider charity sector and where WCR could differentiate. A key question was how other organisations were explaining discovery research to non-specialist audiences, and whether the language being used was helping or hindering. The analysis was delivered as a standalone output; the client found it valuable enough to extend the brief, asking for additional work on emotive messaging specifically.

With both strands complete, I ran a thematic analysis and distilled the findings into a set of clear, non-technical requirements with assigned success metrics. These were presented back to the full stakeholder group. The response confirmed we'd understood the brief accurately, and that the website could go further than they'd expected to address the challenges they'd raised.

(Above) Key themes from the discovery playback deck, (below) actioning point 4 of those takeaways, we updated the donate component to include statements that quantified donation impact, without a product or service outcome.

Definition

Having gathered strong insights throughout discovery, I wanted to ensure we could prioritise and design the most effective scope possible, while also compiling a roadmap of ongoing work to continue addressing challenges after the initial redesign.

I worked closely with the project manager to refine our scope, and designed before-and-after user journeys to map out for clients where pain points existed, both for internal goals and external users, and how we could address them most efficiently.

Once the client had fed into the new user journey designs, I produced a new sitemap to facilitate what was needed. This was reviewed by an external SEO director who had an audit for us to incorporate into our work.

Finally, we wanted to avoid any waste when it came to the insight gathered. Knowing that what we built would only perform as well as its content, we designed additional audience personas combining our research with a market research piece the charity had recently commissioned for their brand and marketing work. This produced a cohesive guide for the charity and its stakeholders that could outlast the project itself, covering UX guidance, pain-point mapping, need mapping, and tone of voice guidance for written content.

Design

Our competitor research showed that cancer discovery research was missing a playful voice that spoke with clarity, and that made content truly accessible. This was the core manifesto that helped keep one consistent thread throughout design. Fun, 3D elements, bright punchy colours and non-intrusive scroll interactions keep the website feeling fresh in a way competitors weren't, driving engagement and inviting a fresh new generation of cure-starters.

Delivery

Throughout the build phase I worked closely with developers, reviewing designs in build and resolving edge cases as a team. The project was built to adhere as closely as possible to WCAG 2.2 AA guidelines.

Outcome

The new website out-performed the old one in both performance and conversion.

It also built a positive and lasting relationship with the client, and two years on we remain their digital partner of choice for UX and web. This means we continue to contribute to ongoing testing and optimisation, including recent work to update their conversion journey, which delivered further conversion gains.
