Fight for Sight x Vision Foundation

Accessible website redesign for a newly merged charity

When two of the UK's leading eye health charities merged, they needed a digital presence that could do something genuinely difficult: serve an audience that includes blind and partially sighted people, their families, and the professionals who support them, all within a single, accessible site. I led UX across the full project, from discovery through to build handover, designing to WCAG 2.2 AA as a baseline and treating accessibility not as a checklist item but as the core design constraint.

The challenge

Two organisations with separate audiences, content estates, and brand identities needed to become one. The Fight for Sight audience skewed toward researchers and donors; the Vision Foundation audience skewed toward people with sight loss and their support networks. Neither site had been built with rigorous accessibility in mind.

The task wasn't just a merger of content. It was a question of who the new site was actually for, and whether we could design it so that a screen reader user and a first-time donor could both complete their goals without friction.

Discovery

To understand what the merged organisation needed to be, we started with the fundamentals: brand values, mission and vision, and what each organisation's stakeholders wanted the new entity to stand for. Stakeholder interviews gave us alignment on strategic direction early, before we touched a pixel.

We ran a content audit across both the Fight for Sight and Vision Foundation websites, which quickly revealed how much duplication existed and how inconsistently similar content was structured. Paired with an SEO and analytics audit, this gave us a clear picture of where traffic was actually coming from, which pages were performing, and what could safely be cut.

Audience understanding was critical on this project in a way it rarely is. We built personas across the full spectrum of users: people with varying degrees of sight loss using assistive technology, carers and family members searching for support resources, donors researching the charity before giving, and researchers looking for grant funding. Those audiences have genuinely different needs and very different relationships with digital interfaces.

We also ran user research testing with 5 participants, including people who rely on screen readers and magnification software day-to-day. Hearing directly from those users in the research phase meant we weren't making assumptions about how assistive technology is actually used in practice.

Competitor and sector research gave us a benchmark for what good looked like in the charitable eye health space, and a moodboard creative direction session aligned stakeholders on visual tone before definition began.

Definition

From the research, we moved into definition. We built a sitemap and IA to consolidate the two content estates into a single, logical structure, resolved duplication, and ensured the navigation hierarchy made sense to someone discovering the charity for the first time. Content modelling mapped out the repeating structures across page types, which informed the component set downstream.

Functionality scoping used the MoSCoW method to prioritise features against timeline and budget, keeping the build realistic. Page templates and components were defined alongside a full build specification covering browser support, hosting, and technical requirements.

Design concept development followed collaborative moodboard feedback with the client. We didn't present a single direction; we built shared understanding of the visual language first, then developed the concept from that agreed foundation.

The phase closed with prototype user testing across 5 participants, again including screen reader users. Testing at this stage let us validate the IA, the component behaviour with assistive tech, and the legibility of our typographic and colour decisions before anything went to build.

404 page design showing desktop and mobile layouts: branded ‘sad eye’ mark, short reassurance copy, and a primary route back to the homepage.

Accessibility as a design constraint

This is the project where accessibility wasn't a retrofit. It shaped decisions from the start. Colour contrast was checked at every stage, not just at sign-off. Interactive components were designed with keyboard navigation and focus states as primary considerations, not afterthoughts. We tested with real screen reader users in both research and definition phases, and the content model was structured to support meaningful heading hierarchies and descriptive link text at a structural level.

WCAG 2.2 AA was the stated standard, but the research conversations pushed us further in several places. When someone tells you they navigate entirely by heading structure, you reconsider every heading decision in the IA.
