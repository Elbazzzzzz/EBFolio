How I use AI as a UX designer

I get asked about this a lot, so I thought I'd write it down properly. The short version is that AI hasn't changed what I do, it's changed how long the slow bits take. The thinking, the judgement, the decisions - those are still mine. But there's a lot of legwork in UX, and that's where AI has quietly become really useful.

Research

Research produces a lot of stuff. Interview transcripts, survey responses, competitor notes. The insights are the valuable part, but getting to them used to mean days of reading, highlighting and moving sticky notes around.

These days I let AI do the first read-through. I'll give it a set of transcripts and ask what themes keep coming up, where people contradict themselves, or whether anything unexpected is hiding in there. Then, and this is the important bit, I go back to the source material and check everything myself. AI is great at spotting patterns but it will happily invent one if you let it, so nothing goes into a findings deck that I haven't traced back to something a real person actually said.

What I've noticed is that I turn up to workshops with better questions now, because my time went into thinking about the findings rather than assembling them.

Spotting accessibility issues in code

I'm not a developer, but I work alongside them every day, and accessibility is something I care about a lot. This is honestly where AI has surprised me most.

Before a build goes to review, I'll run the front-end code through AI and ask it to look for accessibility problems. Missing alt text, wonky heading structures, form fields without labels, colour contrast that's baked into the CSS. Things that used to take an afternoon of poking around now surface in a few minutes.

I still test properly afterwards, with a keyboard and a screen reader, because no tool can tell you what an experience actually feels like for the person using it. But catching the obvious stuff early means the conversations I have with developers are about the genuinely tricky problems, which is a much better use of everyone's time.

Learning new things (without letting it do the learning for me)

This is the bit I'm probably most careful about. It's really easy to ask AI to do something, get a decent result, and learn absolutely nothing in the process. I've fallen into that trap before and it's not a nice feeling.

So I try to flip it around. When I want to understand something new, I ask AI to explain it, then I have a go myself and ask it to tell me where I went wrong. If it writes code for me, I make it talk me through every line until I could explain it to someone else. The little test I use is: could I do this again tomorrow without any help? If the answer is no, I haven't really learned it yet.

It's a slower way to use AI, but it means the skills actually stick.

Where I've landed on all this

I think of AI a bit like a very fast, very keen junior colleague. Brilliant for first drafts and heavy lifting, but you'd never send their work to a client without looking at it first. Everything it gives me gets checked, questioned, and quite often reworked before it goes anywhere near a real project.

That feels like the right balance to me. The tools keep getting better, and I'll keep using them. But the responsibility for the work being good, honest and accessible stays with me, and I wouldn't want it any other way.
