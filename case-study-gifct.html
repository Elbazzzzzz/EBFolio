The Global Internet Forum to Counter Terrorism

Three interconnected research tools for digital counter-terrorism professionals: a searchable definitions framework, a thematic publication browser, and a risk mitigation resource. Built for policy makers, trust-and-safety teams, and legal experts across some of the world's largest platforms.

The Approach

GIFCT needed to turn three complex, data-heavy internal resources into usable online tools. The approach centred on understanding how counter-terrorism practitioners actually research, rather than imposing a structure borrowed from the source materials. Mental models varied significantly across user groups, so the design had to flex without losing rigour.

The Team

I led both UX and UI on this project, supported by a UI designer, alongside engineering and project management. Client stakeholders came from organisations including Facebook, YouTube, Discord, Zoom, Twitter, Tech Against Terrorism, and the Global Network on Extremism and Technology. Their scrutiny was close and their domain expertise was deep.

The Problem

Global definitions of terrorism and violent extremism are fragmented, inconsistently worded, and buried in legislation across dozens of jurisdictions. Practitioners at major tech platforms needed to research and compare them fast, without becoming legal experts. Alongside this, hundreds of academic publications needed to become browsable by topic, and a critical policy resource on terrorist designation lists needed to be navigable enough that people would actually read it in full.

The global definitions research platform on desktop (above) and mobile (below), used by policy makers and trust-and-safety teams to compare terrorism legislation across jurisdictions.

Method

Discovery used stakeholder interviews with practitioner-representative policy makers from Airbnb, Twitter, and Discord. Definition built a sitemap and early scamps grounded in those findings. The final design was built directly in code and tested with the same user group. Minor wording adjustments followed to better reflect the language used by legislation professionals, before launch.

Discovery

With around two months from brief to delivery, I recommended user interviews to close the knowledge gap quickly. GIFCT connected us with policy makers from Airbnb, Twitter, and Discord for two 45-minute sessions. The domain was genuinely unfamiliar territory, so open questions and follow-up probing were essential. Desk research ran in parallel to avoid reinventing any wheels.

Definition

The most complex IA challenge was the thematic tagging framework: seven broad themes, each with subtopics, some with a further layer beneath. With each GNET publication carrying between one and five applicable tags, we needed a filtering approach that matched how practitioners think, not how the taxonomy was structured internally. A sitemap and scamps aligned the three tools into a coherent experience before anything was built.

Design

The interface prioritised scannable structure and fast retrieval. For the definitions framework, filters by jurisdiction and governance type reflected the real comparison tasks practitioners described in interviews. For the tagging browser, progressive disclosure handled the taxonomy depth without overwhelming first-time users. The risk mitigation resource was structured to reward reading in full, not just skimming.

Delivery

The design was built directly in code and tested with the original interview participants. Feedback was positive. Wording adjustments were made to align with the precise language used in legislation, then the tools were launched at GIFCT's global summit.

Outcome

The platform became GIFCT’s most adopted digital release, launched at their global summit. Practitioners reported significantly faster research across all three tools. The project came via a direct recommendation from the International Centre for the Study of Radicalisation at King’s College London, following earlier work our agency had completed for them.
